01

Start with place

Different places, different needs.

Population and geography provide the context for local services. Begin with where residents live and where council spending is concentrated.

From population concentration to spending intensity

The density map establishes a strong contrast between inner Melbourne and sparsely populated regional councils. Melbourne also records the largest total expenditure, at about $715 million. Yet a large total budget does not tell us how much is spent for each resident. The next comparison shifts from total spending to spending per person, revealing a different pattern.

02

Follow the money

One state. Uneven spending.

Governance and recreation & culture account for about 43% of expenditure. Spending per resident also differs substantially between council groups.

The spending pattern changes when population is considered

Governance and recreation & culture account for about 43% of the $12.18 billion spent across councils. Within that overall budget, expenditure per resident is higher in Small Shires: their median is about $3,324, compared with $1,488 for Metro councils. Small Shires also show a wider spread, including West Wimmera at about $8,367. This distribution is consistent with the challenge of serving smaller populations across large areas, although these charts do not isolate the causes of higher costs. To understand what spending means for services, we next examine waste collection and diversion.

03

A closer look at waste

Cost is only part of the picture.

Alpine records the highest landfill diversion in 2024–25, while Melbourne has the highest garbage collection cost per bin. Compare this snapshot with council histories and the changing mix of collected waste.

Waste outcomes change over time, beyond the cost snapshot

The 2024–25 comparison shows that a high collection cost can coexist with a relatively low diversion rate: Melbourne records $475.77 per bin and 28.5% diversion, while Alpine records $112.55 and 74.1%. These examples motivate looking beyond price, rather than proving a statewide relationship. The five-year heatmap adds a local time perspective: in the default Metro view, Knox rises from 52.1% to 71.2% diversion. The longer collection series shows organics overtaking recyclables in 2020–21 and reaching about 718,000 tonnes in 2023–24, while garbage peaks in 2019–20. Together, these records show changes in both diversion and the mix of collected waste; changing reporting coverage limits direct comparisons of annual statewide totals.

04

Beyond the budget

Libraries bring services into use.

Funding, borrowing, visits and attendance describe different parts of a library service. Yarra leads branch visits per resident in 2024–25; Indigo rises from rank 23 to rank 6 over the three years shown.

Library use reveals movement that budgets alone cannot show

The library profiles separate funding, borrowing, visits and attendance, allowing services to differ across measures without combining them into a single score. The ranking chart then introduces change over time. Yarra returns to first place for visits per resident in 2024–25 after ranking third the previous year. Indigo rises from 23rd to sixth; its visits increase from 3.12 to 5.15 per resident, so the movement reflects growth in the underlying measure as well as a change in rank. This leads to the final perspective: where facilities are located and how residents rate service areas.

05

Access and experience

Services matter where people use them.

Physical access adds another perspective to spending and usage. Finish with residents’ ratings: arts & libraries lead the survey’s 28 service areas, while unsealed roads receive the lowest rating.

What the evidence adds up to

Public services vary in cost, use and residents’ experience.

The story begins with geography: dense metropolitan councils and sparsely populated shires serve very different places. That contrast appears again in spending, with the Small Shire median expenditure per resident about 2.2 times the Metro median. This is evidence of uneven spending intensity, rather than a ranking of service quality.

The time series add a second conclusion: service activity is changing. Knox’s landfill diversion increases by 19.1 percentage points over five years, reported organics collection overtakes recyclables, and Indigo’s library visits rise from 3.12 to 5.15 per resident. These examples show why a single-year budget cannot capture the direction of service outcomes and use.

The final maps and ratings bring the story back to people. Listed facility locations show spatial distribution, while the survey reveals a 35-point gap between arts centres & libraries (73) and unsealed roads (38). Taken together, the data support assessing public services through several lenses: local context, spending per resident, change over time, usage and residents’ ratings.
